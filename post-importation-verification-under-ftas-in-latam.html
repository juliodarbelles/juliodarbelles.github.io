Post-Importation Verification Under FTAs in LATAM

The preference is claimed at the border in a single field. The evidence for it sits in a plant in another country, and the question arrives years later.
Julio d’Arbelles
Trade Compliance · Customs Valuation · Rules of Origin
juliodarbelles.github.io

A preference claim takes one field on an entry. The importer states that the good qualifies, the duty comes off, the container moves, and the transaction closes. Everything that makes the claim true happened somewhere else: in a production order, in a bill of materials, in a purchase decision made by someone who has never seen a customs form.

The claim and its proof are separated by a border, by a corporate boundary, and usually by two or three years.

That separation is the whole subject. Post-importation verification is the mechanism by which the importing authority closes the distance, and almost everything that determines the outcome is decided before the first letter arrives.

The three parties, and which one pays

There are three roles in a preferential claim and they rarely sit in the same company. The importer claims the preference and owes the duties. The exporter certifies origin. The producer holds the records that prove it. In an affiliated structure the three may report to the same head office. In an arm's-length sale they may barely know each other.

Under CAFTA-DR and under the USMCA, the importing authority does not have to work through that chain politely. Chapter Four of CAFTA-DR authorizes the customs authority of the importing Party to verify a claim through written requests for information addressed to the importer, the exporter, or the producer, through questionnaires, and through visits to the premises of the exporter or producer, with consent. The USMCA carries the same architecture in Chapter Five. The authority can go straight to the plant, in a country where it has no jurisdiction over anything else, and ask to see the records behind a specific entry.

The European agreement does the opposite, and the contrast is worth holding on to. Under the EU–Central America Association Agreement, verification runs on administrative cooperation. The importing customs sends the proof of origin back to the issuing authority in the exporting country, and that authority conducts the verification and reports back. The Central American producer is examined by its own customs administration, in its own language, under a deadline the two administrations negotiate between themselves.

For a company operating both instruments out of the same plant, that difference is not academic. Under one, a foreign administration may show up at your door. Under the other, your own administration shows up carrying a foreign administration's questions. The preparation is not the same, and neither is the political texture of the visit.

What does not change is who pays. The denial lands on the entry, the entry belongs to the importer, and the duties plus interest are assessed against the importer. When the importer is a customer rather than an affiliate, the producer whose record failed suffers no direct assessment at all. It loses the customer instead.

What arrives, and what to ask for

Verification usually opens with paper. A letter, a questionnaire, a list of entries by number and date, a response window measured in days. The instinct in most organizations is relief. A questionnaire feels containable. It can be answered from an office, by someone who knows the file, without disrupting production.

That instinct is worth resisting.

My first origin verification was in Nicaragua in 2008. I was twenty-four, I had read the customs compendium on rules of origin, and I had never run a determination against real production data. An external adviser took the case, and the first substantive decision we made was to ask the authority for an on-site verification rather than answer the questionnaire from a distance.

It was, in the short term, a terrible decision. It compressed the schedule, it put the plant under observation, and it forced us to reconstruct in weeks what should have been maintained for years. We verified actual consumption production order by production order, thousands of records, and tied each order to the import declaration for the non-originating input and to the sales invoice for the finished good. We did it against the clock because the record had never been assembled that way before.

It was the right decision for a reason that has held up over eighteen years. A questionnaire is judged on the documents you choose to send. A visit is judged on the system that produced them. If the system is sound, the visit is the stronger showing, because the officer sees consistency across records nobody curated for the occasion. If the system is not sound, you will find that out in your own plant, with your own people, rather than in a determination letter. Either result is better than the alternative, which is discovering the gap after the response window has closed.

The deadlines deserve their own line. A verification response window that lapses is not a procedural inconvenience. In most of these instruments, failure to respond within the period, or refusal of access for a visit, is itself a ground to deny the preference. The claim is not evaluated on its merits and found wanting. It is simply denied. I have seen more preferences lost to an unanswered letter than to a bad rule-of-origin analysis.

The record, and the volume problem

What a verification asks for is narrow and deep. Not a policy, not a matrix, not a certificate. The underlying documents for specific entries, and the arithmetic that connects them.

For a tariff-shift rule that means the bill of materials, the classification of each non-originating input, and the evidence of what was actually consumed rather than what the standard formula assumed. For regional value content it means costs, and costs mean the accounting system. For de minimis it means a division whose numerator is the value of the non-originating material that fails the rule and whose denominator is the value of the good sold. That calculation runs invoice by invoice. Without the invoice there is no denominator.

The constraint that breaks companies here is volume, not interpretation. A plant running continuous production generates production orders by the thousand and import declarations by the hundred, and a verification covering two or three years reaches all of them. Retrieving a hundred documents from a physical archive is a task. Retrieving forty thousand is a project that will not finish inside the response window.

That is why I have argued since 2008 that origin defense is a digitization problem wearing the clothes of a legal one. In that first case the plant's ERP already produced production orders as files, so the effort concentrated where the paper actually lived: the customs declarations, whose volume in an audit is punishing, and the export invoices supporting each sale. After the audit closed, the practice stopped being a project. Invoicing scanned and filed as a matter of routine. Purchasing scanned the declarations for that class of material. The customs department scanned its own set as a double check. The business ran the de minimis calculation every month inside the cost review, alongside the other things that move.

None of that is sophisticated. It is the difference between a verification that consumes three weeks and one that cannot be answered at all.

The input you cannot see

There is a structural gap in origin analysis that almost never surfaces in the company's own data, and it deserves more attention than it gets in LATAM operations.

A producer analyzing its exposure starts with its own import statistics. Every non-originating material the company brought in is visible there, classified, valued, dated. The analysis feels complete because the data is complete.

It is not complete, because it does not include what your local suppliers imported.

A supplier in the same country sells you an input, invoices you locally, in local currency, with a local tax identification. Nothing in that transaction announces that the supplier imported the material from outside the region last month. The input is foreign, it may fail the required tariff shift, and it appears nowhere in your import records because it was never your import. Under accumulation rules the material is originating only if it qualifies in its own right, and the supplier is under no automatic obligation to tell you that it does not.

This is where a verification finds things the company did not know about itself. The remedy is unglamorous. Supplier declarations for every locally sourced input that touches a certified product, refreshed on a schedule, with the classification and the origin basis stated rather than asserted. Companies resist this because it is administrative work for a risk that has never materialized. It materializes on the day the officer asks where the resin came from and the answer is a local invoice with nothing behind it.

Why the arithmetic changed in 2026

Until recently, a failed verification on a U.S. entry had a reasonably well understood back door. If the producer or the importer found the problem first, prior disclosure under 19 U.S.C. 1592(c)(4) limited the exposure substantially, and the mitigation practice around penalties left considerable room to negotiate the rest downward.

Executive Order 14411, “Strengthening Customs Enforcement,” signed on 3 June 2026, directed the Secretary to revise all mitigation standards within ninety days. That window closed on 1 September 2026. The order sets a minimum penalty floor of not less than fifty percent of the assessed penalty, absent exceptional circumstances materially affecting national security, establishes a minimum floor for liquidated damages, and eliminates mitigation entirely for repeat offenders. The national security carve-out is written narrowly and is not a general escape.

At the time of writing, CBP has published the enforcement rationale and the implementation notice on Form 5106 accuracy, under which the agency may void an importer of record number for inaccurate or incomplete information beginning 18 September 2026. Whether the revised mitigation guidelines themselves have issued in final form should be confirmed against the Federal Register before anyone relies on the detail.

The direction is clear enough to plan against, and it changes two calculations that matter to anyone certifying origin from Latin America into the United States.

The first is the value of finding your own error. Prior disclosure remains the instrument, and it remains worth using, but the discount at the end of it is smaller. A disclosure that used to be an inexpensive correction is now a decision with a real price attached, which means the analysis behind it has to be better and the timing has to be earlier.

The second is what happens on the second occasion. Elimination of mitigation for repeat offenders converts a first finding into a permanent change in the company's position. Before, a company that lost a verification, paid, and fixed its process was in roughly the same place as any other importer the next time. Now the first determination follows the file. The record you build immediately after a first audit is the thing that keeps a second one from being catastrophic, and that record has to be built while the first one is still fresh, not when the second letter arrives.

What to do before the letter

Verification is not an event that arrives. It is a test of decisions taken years earlier, most of them by people who did not know they were making an origin decision.

The practical implications are few and they are boring, which is why they get postponed. Keep the origin calculation as a live monthly control tied to the current bill of materials rather than a determination made once and filed. Digitize the declarations and the invoices as they are generated, because retrieval under deadline is the failure mode, not analysis. Obtain supplier declarations for locally sourced inputs and refresh them. Know which of your entries sit under which instrument, because the European route and the North American route send different people to different doors. Answer within the window, always, even when the answer is incomplete, because silence converts an arguable case into an automatic denial.

And if the choice is offered, consider asking for the visit. It costs more, it exposes more, and it is the only version of this process in which the officer sees what you actually run rather than what you were able to assemble in twenty days.
