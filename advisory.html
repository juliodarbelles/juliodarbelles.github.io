Advisory · Central America

Three authorizations decide whether your shipment lands.

None of them implies the other. A plant approved in Guatemala is not approved in Honduras. A sanitary registration is not a customs origin determination. And the tariff preference is claimed by your importer, on their entry, against records that sit in your plant. I work the three together, from inside the operation, for U.S. exporters selling into Central America.

01Where shipments actually stop
Layer one

Plant eligibility, country by country

Every Central American authority runs its own establishment register, on its own cycle, with its own documentary standard. Approval in one market carries no weight in the next, and an expired listing stops a container that is otherwise perfect on paper.

I map which of your facilities are listed where, what each authority is missing, and what the renewal calendar looks like across the region so the gaps surface before a purchase order does.

Layer two

Sanitary registration, SKU by SKU

Registration attaches to the product, not to the company, and a reformulation, a label change or a new pack size can require a new file. It authorizes sale in that market and says nothing about customs origin, which is where most exporters conflate two unrelated determinations.

I build and track the registration file per SKU per country, including the label review that keeps the marketing claim consistent with what the customs declaration will say.

Layer three

CAFTA-DR origin qualification

Preference under Chapter 4 is self-certified and claimed by the importer on their entry, but the evidence that supports it lives in your bill of materials, your supplier declarations and your production records. When the importing authority verifies after the fact, it reaches back to the producer.

I run the qualification against the specific rule for your tariff line, document the certification so it survives verification, and re-run it when a formulation, a supplier or a price moves.

The corridor

Transit through Panama without losing the preference

Panama is the natural consolidation point for the region, and it is also where preferential treatment is most often lost. Operations that are entirely lawful in the free zone, repackaging and consolidation among them, can break the customs control continuity that Article 4.12 requires.

Since April 2026 the Panamanian customs authority requires a Reexport Declaration, which for the first time gives importers a formal document to anchor that chain. I structure the transit so the paperwork proves what the treaty asks it to prove.

02How I work
  • Market-entry assessment. One country or the region: what authorizations you hold, what you are missing, and what the realistic timeline is before first shipment.
  • Origin qualification and certification files. Rule-by-rule analysis for your tariff lines, with the documentation built to be defended rather than filed.
  • Transit and regime structuring. Consolidation through Panama, temporary admission and free-zone operations reviewed against what each one costs you in preference.
  • Verification and audit response. When an importing authority reaches back to the producer, the file either holds or it does not. I have built and defended both sides of that record.
  • Standing advisory. A monthly review for exporters with live volume, so a reformulation or a supplier change does not quietly break a certification.
What this is not I am not a customs broker, a freight forwarder or a carrier, and I do not move cargo or quote freight. I work the regulatory and origin file, and I coordinate with the licensed brokers and operators you or I select in each market. The distinction matters, because the exposure I am hired to control lands on the importer of record and on the producer's own documentation, not on the transport leg.
03Why me

Eighteen years inside the operation, not beside it. I administered the temporary admission regime for an export-manufacturing operation shipping ninety-five percent of its production across Central America, ran tariff-rate quota administration when quotas still governed the trade, defended customs audits to settlement, and argued classification and origin files through to published rulings and, in one case, through to an amended annex of a trade agreement.

That range is the point. Plant approvals, sanitary registration, origin qualification and customs regimes are usually four different people in four different firms, each answering only for their own layer. The failures happen between the layers, and they surface months later on someone else's entry.
